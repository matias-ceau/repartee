**mirror:** I would like to start with where you grew up. Where was home when you were small?

**me:** Nantes. Until I was eleven. A small house near the Erdre, with a garden my father was always digging up for no reason

**mirror:** What do you remember first about Nantes?

**me:** The red bike, honestly. I got it for Christmas when I was six. My father had repainted it himself, it was second-hand and a bit too big for me
I fell off it maybe a hundred times that winter

**mirror:** You have mentioned that you cycled to school alone. How old were you?

**me:** Nine. My mother was afraid every morning, she told me much later. My father said it was good for me and he won the argument, which was rare
The route was a quiet street, a footpath behind the swimming pool, and one crossing with a lollipop man, monsieur Le Goff, who knew my name. I think that crossing is the reason I do this job

**mirror:** Why that crossing in particular?

**me:** Because it was the only place where I needed an adult, and the adult was there. Everything else, the street was slow enough for me to manage. I did not know it then, but that is the whole design brief
To be fair, I only understood that at university

**mirror:** What did you do with your father, apart from the bike?

**me:** Football. FC Nantes, at the Beaujoire, on Saturdays. I was seven or eight. He explained offside to me with sugar sachets at the bar before the match
I never understood offside. I understood that it was our moment, just the two of us, because Hugo was too small to come

**mirror:** Hugo is your younger brother?

**me:** Yes, four years younger. When we were small he followed me everywhere and I found it very annoying. Now he cooks for me whenever I go to Bordeaux, so I think he won

**mirror:** Tell me about the sea. Where does that start?

**me:** Pornic, with Mamie Jeanne, my father's mother. Every summer. She taught me to swim in the sea when I was five, in water at maybe 17 degrees, holding me under the belly and counting in Breton
She still swims. She went in on the morning of her 90th birthday, before all of us

**mirror:** Then you moved. What happened?

**me:** Summer 2004. My mother got a post at a primary school in Rennes and we all moved. I was eleven and I hated it, really hated it, for about a year
I had lost my route to school. That sounds strange but that is how it felt. In Rennes my mother drove me for the first months because she did not know the streets, and I felt like a parcel

**mirror:** How did it get better?

**me:** I made her a map. A real one, with coloured pencils, of a route by bike with the crossings marked, and I showed her that it was safe. She let me go after that
I think that was my first planning project. I was twelve

**mirror:** That is a lovely story. Is there anything from those years you still carry with you, in how you live now?

**me:** Two things, I think. That a child alone on a bike is the best test of a street. And that when I am scared of a change I make a list, or a map, and then I go and do it anyway
Also galettes. My father's leek tart. That is three things, sorry

**mirror:** Thank you. Is there anything I should not have assumed, or something you want to correct?

**me:** You said "where was home" at the start. Home was Nantes until eleven, then it took a long time for it to be anywhere. Maybe Bristol now. I am not sure yet
