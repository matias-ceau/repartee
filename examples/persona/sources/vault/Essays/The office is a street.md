# The office is a street

On Tuesday mornings the second floor of our office smells of toast and wet coats, and for the first hour almost nothing gets done; people stand by the kettle, someone complains about the coffee machine that has been broken since 2019, a junior asks a senior what a swept path analysis is for, and the senior, who was about to go to a meeting, puts her bag down and draws it on the back of an envelope. I used to think of that hour as waste. I now think it is most of the point.

When the lockdowns came I worked from my kitchen in Lyon for the better part of a year, drawing cycle lanes on a laptop balanced on a chopping board, and I was — I should be honest — perfectly productive. I was also, by the end, a slightly worse planner than I had been, and I did not notice it until I was back in a room with other people and realised how many of my ideas had come from overhearing theirs.

The argument for working fully from home is strong, and I do not want to caricature it. Commutes are long and expensive; offices are often loud, ugly and designed for nobody in particular; parents and carers gain hours they cannot get any other way. For an experienced person who knows the work and the people, a quiet room and a good connection can be better than any office I have known. A colleague of mine — who would rather I did not use his name — told me he did the best analysis of his life in his spare bedroom, and I believe him.

But the experienced person is not the only person in the building. The people who lose most from a fully remote office are the ones who have the least voice in deciding it: juniors, newcomers, people who moved to a new country for the job and do not yet know who to ask. Knowledge in a firm like ours is not stored in documents; it is stored in conversations that nobody schedules. You cannot book a meeting to ask a question you do not yet know you have.

This is where my work leaks into my opinions, as it always does. A good office works like a good street; it is valuable less for its destinations than for the unplanned encounters between them. A street that people only drive through has no life, however efficient it is, and an organisation that people only log into is much the same. The kettle is a bench; the corridor is a pavement; the long table where people eat lunch is a square.

So my position, which pleases nobody completely, is three days. Three days in the office, chosen together so that the building is actually full when people come in, and two days at home for the work that needs silence. It is not a principle so much as a design; like most designs, it will be wrong for someone, and it should be adjusted when it is.

I have changed my mind about enough things to hold this one loosely. What I am sure of is smaller. In my first month in Bristol, lost in acronyms and in English, the person who saved me was the one at the next desk, who noticed my face during a meeting and explained, afterwards and without being asked, what a low traffic neighbourhood was. I had designed a dozen of them in Lyon; I simply did not know their English name. Nobody would have noticed that on a video call.

So I still go in.
