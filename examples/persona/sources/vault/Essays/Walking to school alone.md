# Walking to school alone

At ten past eight on a weekday in Easton, the street outside my window belongs to cars that are not going anywhere; they idle in a line behind a van, their engines warm, each of them carrying one child about eight hundred metres to a school gate that most of those children could reach on foot in eleven minutes. I count them sometimes, with my tea, the way other people count birds.

When I was nine, in Nantes, I cycled to school alone. It was a red bike my father had repainted himself, a little too big for me, and the route was a quiet residential street, a footpath behind the swimming pool and one crossing with a lollipop man who knew my name. My mother was afraid every morning — she told me so, years later — and my father said it was good for me. He was right, though not for the reason he thought. It was not good for my legs or my character; it was good for my sense that the city was mine, that I could read it and move through it without anyone's permission.

I have spent my working life, first in Lyon and now in Bristol, designing streets, and I have come to think that there is one test which contains most of the others. Can a nine-year-old walk or cycle to school on it alone? If the answer is yes, the street is probably slow enough, narrow enough at the crossings, overlooked by enough windows and shops, and connected to enough other quiet streets; if the answer is no, it usually fails at least one of those, and adding a painted line will not save it.

The test is useful precisely because it is not about children. A street that a nine-year-old can cross is a street that an eighty-year-old can cross, and a street where parents feel safe letting go is a street where a woman walking home at eleven at night feels safe too. Children are simply the most honest instrument we have; they are small, they are distractible, and they have not yet learned to compensate for bad design by being afraid.

There is a familiar objection, which I hear in nearly every consultation: the danger is not the street but the strangers. I understand it, and I do not dismiss it, but the numbers say something else; the thing most likely to hurt a child on the way to school is a car, very often one driven by another parent on the way to the same school. We have made the streets dangerous by driving children through them to protect them from the streets.

So the work, in practice, is unglamorous. School streets that close for forty minutes in the morning and afternoon; continuous pavements across side roads, so that the car is the one that has to ask; crossings placed where children actually cross rather than where the traffic model would like them to; and, above all, patience with the parents, who are not villains but people doing the reasonable thing in an unreasonable place.

I do not think every child should walk to school alone, and I do not think parents who drive are doing anything wrong. What I think is narrower and more stubborn: that the choice should be real. At the moment, for most of the children idling under my window, it is not; the street has already made it for them.

Last month, near the Railway Path, I watched a girl of about nine get off her bike at the crossing, look both ways twice, and push across with enormous seriousness, as if the whole city were watching. Nobody was watching except me. She did not need anyone.

That is the whole point.
