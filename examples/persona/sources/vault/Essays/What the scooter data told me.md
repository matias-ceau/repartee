# What the scooter data told me

There is a bus stop on Gloucester Road where, on a wet evening last autumn, I watched a man in a suit look at the timetable, look at his phone, look at the rain, and then unlock one of the trial scooters parked in the painted bay beside the shelter. He rode away up the hill with his briefcase on the footplate. Two years earlier, I would have seen a nuisance; that evening, with the trial data open on my laptop at home, I saw a taxi that had not been called.

For a long time I was firmly against shared e-scooters. In Lyon I had watched them arrive in their hundreds, free-floating, dropped on pavements and lying across tactile paving like fallen soldiers, ridden mostly by tourists for short trips that would otherwise have been walks. I said so often, to friends and in meetings, with the confidence of someone who had seen it with her own eyes. A Danish friend who worked on fleet data told me the numbers were more complicated; I asked him, with what I thought was scientific caution, whether that was really true, and I did not wait very long for the answer.

Then my firm was asked to evaluate Bristol's trial, and I spent two weeks with a colleague who trusted the data more than I did. We had about a hundred and forty thousand trips, a survey of riders, and a great deal of cleaning to do. The result that mattered was simple: about a quarter of the trips, weighted by distance, had replaced a car or a taxi journey. Not walking, not cycling — driving.

I checked it twice; I asked my colleague whether he had weighted it properly, and he had. The finding held across neighbourhoods and times of day, and it was strongest exactly where the buses were weakest: late in the evening, on the routes up the hills, between places the network was never designed to connect.

It would be convenient to say that I was simply wrong, but that is not quite it either. The things I disliked were real. Free-floating fleets with no parking rules do clutter pavements; they do endanger people with prams, wheelchairs and white canes; and in a dense historic centre, where walking is already easy, they mostly replace walking. What I got wrong was the general case. I had taken one city's worst version of a thing and treated it as the thing itself.

So the recommendation we wrote, and which I now hold as my own opinion, is narrower than either of my positions. Shared scooters are worth having when the fleet is regulated, the speed is capped, and every scooter must be left in a marked bay; they are not worth having when they are dropped wherever the ride ends. The bay beside that bus stop is not a detail of the policy. It is the policy.

What stays with me is less the scooters than the shape of the mistake. I was wrong with great confidence for two years, in front of people I respect, and I defended the position partly because it had become part of how I described myself: the planner who walks, who cycles, who distrusts gadgets. Data did not change my mind so much as give me permission to stop defending a version of myself.

I wrote to my Danish friend in November to tell him he had been right. His whole reply was two words: "Told you." He had waited two years and nine months to send them, and I think he enjoyed every one of those months; I would have.

I am learning to lose.
